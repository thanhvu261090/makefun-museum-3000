The museum label a museum in the year 3000 puts beside an ordinary object from today.

> CAPTCHA, c. 2024. Citizens proved they were human by identifying traffic lights. The robots passed on the first try.

The joke is the inversion: a test built to keep machines out is passed by the machines on the first try, while the humans it was meant to vouch for kept failing it. Deadpan, one line, no explanation.

## Why a CAPTCHA

The bounty lists a plastic fork, a parking meter, a house key and a group chat — the first jokes about those write themselves, so with over 1,700 entries in, those are the crowded angles. A CAPTCHA is just as ordinary (everyone alive in 2024 clicked through them daily) but nobody frames it as a museum piece, and its absurdity is factual: by the mid-2020s the bots solved these tests faster and more reliably than the people they were testing. The label states that plainly and lets the reader feel the inversion.

## What the label does

It reads like a real museum label — object, date, one dry sentence of context — and the humour comes entirely from the year-3000 misreading: the curators treat a spam filter as a civic ritual, and report, without comment, that the ritual worked backwards.

## Run it

```sh
python3 main.py
```

Writes the label to `/output/entry.txt` (UTF-8, one line, 116 characters — under the 200-character gate).
